Worked example / 03
A long list of tools can hide a small, specific job.
The request says “Klaviyo expert”. The team actually needs someone to repair two flows, keep a campaign calendar moving and catch mistakes before messages go out. Those responsibilities should appear before a list of platforms.
A useful version of the requirement
“Own the welcome and post-purchase rebuild. Agree entry, exit and suppression rules with the ecommerce lead. Prepare campaigns for review, check the exact version approved, and leave the team with readable delivery notes.”
That wording gives the candidate work to discuss. It also helps the manager decide whether one person can reasonably own it.
Ask for evidence in the work itself.
In a 30-minute first interview, spend five minutes on the operating context, fifteen on a relevant project, five on an awkward edge case, and five on the candidate’s questions.
For the project discussion, ask the candidate to walk through something they are allowed to share. What did they personally own? Which audience rule was difficult? What changed after review? A redacted explanation is enough; confidential customer data is not required.
A specific question: “A customer buys after email one but before email two. Show me where the flow decides whether email two should still send.”
Keep the handoff short and useful.
Put this in the introductionWhy the manager needs it
Relevant work and the person’s contributionGives the interview somewhere concrete to start.

Before a take-home task
Decide whether the work discussion already answers the question. If an exercise is needed, give candidates the same brief, state the time limit, use a bounded task unrelated to live client delivery, and agree how you will assess it before reading submissions.
Record observed evidence separately from interpretation. “Explained how purchase events change eligibility” is more useful feedback than “good cultural fit”. The hiring team remains responsible for the decision.
